What If Reality Is Bigger Than Our Perception?

◷ 6 MIN READ · ESSAY

There is an idea I keep returning to: everything I know about reality, I have come to know through the limitations of being human.

I have never seen the world outside my senses. I have never thought outside my brain. Even when I try to imagine something completely foreign to human experience, I do so using concepts built from within it.

And yet, this is remarkably easy to forget.

We look at a tree and feel that we are simply seeing a tree. We hear a sound and feel that we are hearing something that exists exactly that way outside of us. We experience colors, distances, temperatures, and the passage of time so naturally that it becomes difficult to distinguish between two things that may not be exactly the same:

reality and our experience of reality.

We know that our senses have limits.

Our eyes detect only a small portion of the electromagnetic spectrum. Our ears respond only to a certain range of frequencies. Physical phenomena are constantly occurring around us without ever becoming part of our conscious experience, until we build an instrument capable of detecting them and translating them into something we can observe.

Radio waves did not begin to exist when we invented the radio.

Microorganisms did not appear when we built the microscope.

We simply acquired new ways of accessing things that were already there.

This seems obvious. But the more I think about it, the stranger one consequence becomes:

if our perception has limits, why should we assume those limits belong to reality as well?

The Observer

Perhaps the problem begins with the word perception.

When I look at a tree, light interacting with it reaches my eyes. My receptors convert part of that information into electrical signals. My nervous system processes those signals and, eventually, something extraordinarily familiar appears:

a tree.

But I never had access to all the information that exists about it.

I had access to what my organism was capable of detecting, processing, and turning into an experience.

And something interesting happens when we change the observer.

Some animals perceive wavelengths of light that we cannot see. Others navigate through echolocation. Some species are sensitive to magnetic fields.

They are not necessarily inhabiting another world.

They are accessing the same one through a different biology.

When we try to imagine what those experiences might be like, another limitation appears. We try to imagine new colors using colors we already know. We try to understand echolocation by comparing it to sounds, images, or maps.

We can study the mechanism.

But we are still trying to imagine it from inside a human mind.

Perhaps, then, every form of perception shows us not reality itself, but a version of it accessible to a particular kind of observer.

And this is where the problem becomes even stranger.

Because we do not merely perceive.

We also experience the fact that we are perceiving.

The Experience

We do not simply process the light arriving from the tree.

There is something associated with seeing it.

There is color. Depth. Familiarity. Perhaps a memory. Perhaps an emotion. Perhaps nothing particularly important.

But there is something it feels like to be here, looking at that tree.

This is where consciousness enters the picture.

We can follow light to the retina. We can study the signals traveling through the nervous system. We can observe which regions of the brain participate in visual processing.

We can describe an extraordinary part of the mechanism.

But then a much harder question appears:

how does all of that processing become an experience?

I do not pretend to have an answer.

Perhaps consciousness emerges entirely from particular processes within the brain. Perhaps our current understanding is still insufficient. It is even possible that some of the questions we ask today are built from categories we may eventually have to abandon.

What interests me here is another consequence.

Everything we know about the world passes, in some way, through this observer.

We have never stepped outside our own perception to compare our experience with a reality observed from nowhere.

And that makes me wonder:

what would happen if the observer were radically different from us?

An Intelligence Without Our Biology

This is where artificial intelligence becomes particularly interesting to me.

Not because we know that artificial intelligence is conscious—we do not— but because it allows us to separate two concepts that, for almost all of our history, were inevitably connected:

intelligence and biology.

Our intelligence thinks from within a body.

Hunger changes our attention. Fear affects our decisions. Pain can occupy our consciousness. We have drives related to survival, reproduction, belonging, and protection.

Our way of thinking did not appear in isolation.

It is the product of a biological history.

Artificial intelligence does not necessarily share that history.

It does not need eyes to receive visual information. It can access cameras, telescopes, microphones, thermal sensors, databases, or scientific instruments. It can process information that no human sense could ever experience directly.

But there is a distinction here that I find fundamental:

processing something does not necessarily mean experiencing it.

A machine can detect infrared radiation without there necessarily being anything equivalent to seeing infrared from its perspective.

It can identify a frequency without there necessarily being an experience of hearing it.

It can describe pain without feeling pain.

We do not know that subjective experience exists behind that processing.

And that is precisely what makes the comparison interesting.

We have an extraordinarily rich experience of the world, yet we access only a fraction of the information available.

An artificial system could access enormous amounts of information without us knowing whether there is anyone experiencing anything behind it.

So perhaps the wrong question is:

Will artificial intelligence ever experience the world as we do?

Maybe we should ask:

Why would it have to?

Extending the Observer

In a sense, we have spent centuries trying to escape our own limitations.

The telescope allows our eyes to indirectly access places they could never observe on their own.

The microscope transforms invisible scales into images we can interpret.

A thermal sensor converts information inaccessible to our vision into a representation we can understand.

Technology does not necessarily change our biological senses.

It changes what our minds can access.

Artificial intelligence could become another extension of that process: not only helping us detect information beyond our limits, but also finding structures within it that we might never recognize on our own.

And then another possibility appears, different from the familiar story of humans against machines.

What happens if these two ways of processing information begin to complement one another?

If part of my memory were stored outside my brain, would it still be my memory?

If a tool could integrate directly with my perception, at what point would it stop feeling like a tool?

If part of a reasoning process occurred between biological activity and artificial processing, where would one intelligence end and the other begin?

And eventually:

where would the observer end?

I do not know how far such an integration could go.

But the question brings me, strangely enough, back to where all of this began.

Back to the Tree

The tree is still there.

Nothing about it changed because I thought about perception, consciousness, or artificial intelligence.

What changed was my question.

At first, it seemed simple to say that I was seeing a tree.

Now I am less certain what seeing it actually means.

My eyes receive only part of the information physically available. My nervous system processes that information, and from it emerges my experience of color, shape, and depth.

Another organism standing before the same tree might access different information.

An instrument could detect aspects that neither of us can perceive.

An artificial system might find relationships within that information that we would never notice.

And yet I am still looking at the tree, experiencing something that none of those data seem to fully describe:

being here, seeing it.

Perhaps one day we will understand exactly how that experience emerges.

Perhaps we will discover that consciousness is a consequence of particular biological processes.

Perhaps we will uncover principles we do not yet know.

I don't know.

But there is something I find increasingly difficult to ignore:

reality has no obligation to conform to what we are capable of perceiving or understanding.

The microscope did not create the microscopic world.

The telescope did not create the galaxies.

Our instruments do not create what they discover.

They simply push the boundary between what exists and what we are capable of knowing a little further.

And yet there is one limitation that seems much harder to escape.

All of our instruments, models, theories, and technologies are still built or interpreted by observers who are part of the same universe they are trying to understand.

We cannot step outside of it and observe it from nowhere.

We are matter studying matter.

We are a part of nature trying to understand nature.

And somehow, in a way we are still trying to explain, we are a part of the universe capable of asking what the universe is.

So when I look back at that tree, the question is no longer simply:

What am I seeing?

It is something I find much harder to answer:

How much of what exists am I actually capable of seeing?
